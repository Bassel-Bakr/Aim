Switching is moving your crosshair from a target you've just cleared to the next one. You close that
gap quickly, without losing the accuracy you need to land the next click.

- **Speed:** the transition is the hard part. Targets move too little to matter.
- **Evasive:** targets keep moving after you arrive. Finish with a beat of tracking.
- **Stability:** targets hop in place. Your crosshair has to hold through the hop.
- **Accuracy before speed.** Widen spacing only once you land most switches.

[Aimlabs](../resources/trainers/aimlabs.md) calls it "the heart of dynamic decision-making in
aiming," where focus transfers from target to target in a swift, seamless motion.[^aimlabs-intro]

Two things make one switch harder than another: how far apart the targets sit, and how much they
move once you arrive. Distance sets how fast the transition has to be. Target motion decides how
much work is left after the flick lands.

Aimlabs splits switching two ways, static and dynamic.[^aimlabs-intro]
[Voltaic](../resources/communities/voltaic.md)'s Season 5 KovaaK's benchmark names the three cases
below speed, evasive, and stability.[^voltaic-s5]

## Speed switching

Speed switching puts the weight on the transition itself: how fast you can cross the gap and still
land the click. The targets aren't necessarily frozen, but they move little enough that holding one
is never the hard part.

Good execution is a flick straight to the next target, then a quick micro-adjustment to settle on
it. A slow drift across the gap is the opposite.

The common mistake is chasing raw speed before the flick itself is accurate. That raises your miss
rate faster than it raises your kill rate. The
[speed-accuracy trade-off](../fundamentals/how-aim-works.md) applies directly here.

Train it by widening target spacing gradually, not by starting at a spacing where you can't land most
switches. Treat accuracy, not time, as the number to improve first.

## Evasive switching

Evasive switching is the moving case. The targets keep moving after you switch onto them, so a good
switch still needs a beat of tracking to close it out.

Aimlabs' guidance is to start tracking a target as it changes direction, which gives the best chance
to stay smooth and finish it off. It also advises against pulling off a target too early once
you've committed to it.[^aimlabs-apex-moving]

The common mistake is exactly that: bailing on a target the instant it gets harder to hit, and
jumping to an easier one. That trades one clean kill for a scattered miss.

Train it on scenarios with clearly telegraphed target motion, before moving on to more erratic
movement.

## Stability switching

Stability switching sits between the two. Voltaic added it in Season 5 as a hybrid category
"bridging traditional subcategory gaps."[^voltaic-s5]

The targets barely move at all, hopping or jittering slightly in place. It looks like switching
between stationary targets, but the crosshair has to hold through that small movement instead of
arriving and stopping.

Good execution is a stable flick onto each target that holds through the hop, instead of one that
immediately drifts off.

The common mistake is treating the switch as finished the moment the crosshair arrives, then losing
the target as soon as it moves.

Because this blends switching with tracking, alternate drills between wide-spaced stationary
switching and [Tracking](tracking.md) at a slower pace. That builds both halves before you combine
them.

**Do this next.** Widen target spacing only once you land most switches at the current one.
