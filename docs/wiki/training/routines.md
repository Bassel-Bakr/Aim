A routine is simply the specific set of scenarios you run in a session, in some order, for some
length of time. [Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md) covers why
deliberate, weakness-targeted practice beats just logging hours; this page covers how to actually
assemble a session and a rotation of scenarios around that idea.

## Explanation

**Warm-up.** Repetitive mouse movement and a long static posture build up strain on the same
joints and tendons over a session, and [Aimlabs](../resources/trainers/aimlabs.md) argues a short
stretch or warm-up pass belongs inside the routine itself rather than sitting outside it.[^aimlabs-body]
In practice this means starting on an easier, slower version of your usual scenarios instead of
jumping straight into your hardest setting cold.

**Focus block.** This is the main part of the session: scenarios chosen because
[Benchmarks](benchmarks.md) or your own results point to them as your current weakest subcategory,
run at a difficulty where your technique holds up rather than one you can barely survive, see
[Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md).

**Review.** Closing a session by looking back at what happened, a replay, a stat screen, or a
couple of minutes of honest self-assessment, turns a session into feedback you can act on next
time instead of just more repetitions. Voltaic frames this kind of after-the-fact analysis as
belonging to a separate "Learning Zone," distinct from staying focused and uncritical while you're
actually training.[^volt-mentality]

**Session length.** There's no single correct length, but general motor-learning research favors
several shorter sessions with real rest between them over one marathon session, both for how well
a skill sticks and for how fatigue affects performance while training, see
[Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md). If your accuracy is dropping late
in a session and still down the next day, treat that as a cue to end sooner rather than push
through it.

## Choosing scenarios for your weaknesses

Your weakest area is whichever [Clicking](../skills/clicking.md), [Tracking](../skills/tracking.md),
or [Switching](../skills/switching.md) subcategory scores lowest relative to the others on a
benchmark, not necessarily whichever one feels hardest in the moment, see
[Benchmarks](benchmarks.md). Build your focus block from scenarios in that subcategory, and keep at
least a little time on the others so a fixed weakness doesn't quietly become a new one, per the
variety-over-repetition point in [Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md).

## Using existing playlists

You don't need to build a routine from nothing. Both [Voltaic](../resources/communities/voltaic.md)
and [Revosect](../resources/communities/revosect.md) publish ready-made routines. Voltaic splits
its routines into fundamental routines for general mouse control, game-specific routines for the
aiming your particular game leans on most, and weakness-specific routines layered on top of
whichever of those you're already running.[^volt-start] Revosect
covers similar ground with foundational and weakness-specific routines plus separate game-specific
playlists for a wide range of shooters. Starting from one of these and adjusting it as you learn
your own weak points is a reasonable default instead of guessing at scenario selection from
scratch.

## How often to change a routine

Change a routine when it's stopped teaching you anything, not on a fixed schedule. If you're
consistently hitting a scenario cleanly at its current difficulty, that's the signal to raise the
difficulty or swap in a harder scenario for the same weakness rather than keep repeating a level
you've already mastered, following the same progression logic covered in
[Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md). If a re-run
[benchmark](benchmarks.md) shows your former weak subcategory has caught up with the others,
that's the signal to rebuild the routine around whatever is now your lowest score instead.

## Common mistakes

- Skipping the warm-up and going straight into your hardest scenarios, then blaming a rough start
  on lack of skill instead of a cold session.
- Building a routine entirely around scenarios you already enjoy or are already good at, the same
  score-chasing problem covered in [Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md).
- Never revisiting the routine once it's built, so it keeps targeting a weakness you've already
  fixed.
