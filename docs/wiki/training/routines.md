A routine is the specific set of scenarios you run in a session, in some order, for some length of
time. This page covers how to assemble one.

- **Warm up first.** Start on easier, slower versions of your usual scenarios.
- **Build the focus block around your weakest subcategory.** Pick it from a benchmark, not a feeling.
- **End with a review.** A replay or stat screen turns reps into feedback.
- **Change it when it stops teaching you.** Not on a fixed schedule.

[Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md) covers why deliberate,
weakness-targeted practice beats just logging hours.

## Explanation

**Warm-up.** Repetitive mouse movement and a long static posture build up strain on the same joints
and tendons over a session.

[Aimlabs](../resources/trainers/aimlabs.md) argues a short stretch or warm-up belongs inside the
routine itself, rather than sitting outside it.[^aimlabs-body]

In practice, start on an easier, slower version of your usual scenarios, instead of jumping straight
into your hardest setting cold.

**Focus block.** This is the main part of the session. Pick scenarios because
[Benchmarks](benchmarks.md) or your own results point to them as your current weakest subcategory.

Run them at a difficulty where your technique holds up, not one you can barely survive. See
[Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md).

**Review.** Close a session by looking back at what happened: a replay, a stat screen, or a couple of
minutes of honest self-assessment. That turns a session into feedback you can act on, instead of
just more repetitions.

Voltaic frames this kind of after-the-fact analysis as belonging to a separate "Learning Zone." That's
distinct from staying focused and uncritical while you're actually training.[^volt-mentality]

**Session length.** There's no single correct length. General motor-learning research favors several
shorter sessions with real rest over one marathon session.

That holds both for how well a skill sticks and for how fatigue affects performance while training.
See [Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md).

If your accuracy drops late in a session and stays down the next day, end sooner rather than push
through.

## Choosing scenarios for your weaknesses

Your weakest area is whichever [Clicking](../categories/clicking.md),
[Tracking](../categories/tracking.md), or [Switching](../categories/switching.md) subcategory scores
lowest relative to the others on a benchmark. That's not necessarily the one that feels hardest in
the moment. See [Benchmarks](benchmarks.md).

Build your focus block from scenarios in that subcategory. Keep at least a little time on the others,
so a fixed weakness doesn't quietly become a new one. That's the variety-over-repetition point in
[Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md).

## Using existing playlists

You don't need to build a routine from nothing. Both [Voltaic](../resources/communities/voltaic.md)
and [Revosect](../resources/communities/revosect.md) publish ready-made routines.

Voltaic splits its routines three ways:

- fundamental routines for general mouse control
- game-specific routines for the aiming your particular game leans on most
- weakness-specific routines, layered on top of whichever of those you're already
  running[^volt-start]

Revosect covers similar ground: foundational and weakness-specific routines, plus separate
game-specific playlists for a wide range of shooters.

Starting from one of these and adjusting it as you learn your weak points is a reasonable default.
It beats guessing at scenario selection from scratch.

## How often to change a routine

Change a routine when it's stopped teaching you anything, not on a fixed schedule.

If you consistently hit a scenario cleanly at its current difficulty, raise the difficulty or swap in
a harder scenario for the same weakness. Don't keep repeating a level you've already mastered. That's
the progression logic in [Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md).

If a re-run [benchmark](benchmarks.md) shows your former weak subcategory has caught up with the
others, rebuild the routine. Build it around whatever is now your lowest score.

## Common mistakes

- Skipping the warm-up and going straight into your hardest scenarios. A rough start then gets blamed
  on lack of skill instead of a cold session.
- Building a routine entirely around scenarios you already enjoy or are already good at. That's the
  same score-chasing problem covered in [Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md).
- Never revisiting the routine once it's built, so it keeps targeting a weakness you've already fixed.
