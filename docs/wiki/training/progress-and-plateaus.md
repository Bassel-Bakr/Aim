Progress in aim training rarely moves in a straight line: expect fast early gains that slow down
over time, and expect the occasional stretch where your score barely moves at all. This page
covers what that normal pattern looks like, what usually causes a longer stall, a plateau, and
what actually helps you past one.

## Explanation

**Normal progress patterns.** Early practice on a new scenario tends to produce large, fast gains,
and those gains shrink as you get more skilled: general motor-learning research describes an early
stage of learning marked by large, inconsistent jumps in performance, followed by a slower stage
of smaller gains spent on conscious adjustment and refinement, a pattern that isn't specific to aim
training.[^hk-stages]
Eventually most learners hit a
stretch some researchers call a "learning plateau": performance holds roughly steady for a while
no matter how consistently you show up and train, which motor-learning research treats as an
ordinary, expected stage rather than evidence that something in your training has broken.[^plateau-study] Knowing a
stall is expected doesn't fix it on its own, but it does mean a flat week or two isn't evidence
you've hit a hard ceiling.

**Tracking scores over time.** A single benchmark run is noisy: a bad night's sleep or an off day
can move your score as much as real progress does, so judge your training by the trend across
several [benchmark](benchmarks.md) runs spaced weeks apart rather than any one result. Keeping your
own record of scores makes that trend easier to see, and easier to tell apart from ordinary
week-to-week variance before you start changing your training in response to what might just be
noise.

## Causes of plateaus

**Fatigue.** Training hard without enough recovery between sessions can tip into overreaching, and
if that continues, into overtraining syndrome, a state where performance actually declines rather
than just stalling.[^overtraining] A
plateau that shows up alongside feeling generally run down, not just a flat score, is worth
treating as a fatigue problem first.

**One-sided practice.** Running the same handful of scenarios repeatedly can make you better at
those specific scenarios without making you better at aiming in general, since the skill stops
being challenged once you've adapted to that narrow input;
[Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md) covers this same point through
Revosect's own advice against training only on benchmark scenarios.

**No technique focus.** A plateau can also come from optimizing the wrong thing: pushing volume or
difficulty on a scenario without checking whether your underlying technique, smoothness, timing,
positioning, is actually improving. See [Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md)
on deliberate practice and [How Aim Works](../fundamentals/how-aim-works.md) for what that
underlying technique consists of.

## Ways past a plateau

**Switch focus.** If your current weak subcategory has stopped moving, shift the bulk of your
[routine](routines.md) to a different subcategory for a while rather than grinding the same one
harder; you may come back to the original one able to make faster progress on it once it isn't the
only thing you're doing. **Rest days.** Since fatigue is one of the more common causes of a stall,
a deliberate stretch of lighter training or full rest is a reasonable first thing to try before
restructuring anything else, per the same overtraining research above. **Sensitivity or scenario
variation.** [Voltaic](https://blog.voltaic.gg/muscle-memory/)'s piece on the "muscle memory" myth
lists a deliberate sensitivity change among the ways players work past a stall, on the logic that
starting over at a new sensitivity puts you back at a point where there's obvious room to improve,
rather than at a setting your hand has already fully adjusted to; see
[Sensitivity](../getting-started/sensitivity.md) for more on when that trade-off is worth making.
The same logic extends to swapping in different scenarios for the same skill: a new one forces
your hand to adapt again instead of coasting on one you've already mastered.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a single bad benchmark run as proof you've plateaued, or a single good one as proof
  you've broken through.
- Responding to a fatigue-driven plateau by training harder instead of resting.
- Repeating the exact same scenarios indefinitely and assuming a flat score means you've hit your
  ceiling, rather than that your practice has stopped varying.
