Progress in aim training rarely moves in a straight line. Expect fast early gains that slow down,
and the occasional stretch where your score barely moves at all.

- **Slowing gains are normal.** Early jumps shrink as you get more skilled.
- **A flat stretch isn't evidence of a ceiling.** Plateaus are an expected stage of learning.
- **Judge the trend, not one run.** A single benchmark result is noisy.
- **Rest first if you're run down.** Then vary your focus, scenarios, or sensitivity.

This page covers that normal pattern, what usually causes a longer stall, and what actually helps
you past one.

## Explanation

**Normal progress patterns.** Early practice on a new scenario tends to produce large, fast gains,
and those gains shrink as you get more skilled.

General motor-learning research describes an early stage of large, inconsistent jumps in
performance. A slower stage of smaller gains follows, spent on conscious adjustment and refinement.
The pattern isn't specific to aim training.[^REF-28]

Eventually most learners hit a stretch some researchers call a "learning plateau." Performance holds
roughly steady for a while, however consistently you show up and train.

Motor-learning research treats this as an ordinary, expected stage, not evidence that something in
your training has broken.[^REF-29]

Knowing a stall is expected doesn't fix it on its own. But it does mean a flat week or two isn't
evidence you've hit a hard ceiling.

**Tracking scores over time.** A single benchmark run is noisy. A bad night's sleep or an off day can
move your score as much as real progress does.

So judge your training by the trend across several [benchmark](benchmarks.md) runs spaced weeks
apart, rather than any one result.

Keeping your own record of scores makes that trend easier to see. It also helps you tell it apart
from ordinary week-to-week variance, before you change your training in response to what might just
be noise.

## Causes of plateaus

**Fatigue.** Training hard without enough recovery between sessions can tip into overreaching. If
that continues, it can tip further into overtraining syndrome, where performance actually declines
rather than just stalling.[^REF-46]

A plateau that shows up alongside feeling generally run down, not just a flat score, is worth
treating as a fatigue problem first.

**One-sided practice.** Running the same handful of scenarios repeatedly can make you better at those
scenarios, without making you better at aiming in general.

The skill stops being challenged once you've adapted to that narrow input.
[Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md) covers the same point through
Revosect's own advice against training only on benchmark scenarios.

**No technique focus.** A plateau can also come from optimizing the wrong thing. That means pushing
volume or difficulty on a scenario without checking whether your underlying technique is improving:
smoothness, timing, positioning.

See [Practice Principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md) on deliberate practice, and
[How Aim Works](../fundamentals/how-aim-works.md) for what that underlying technique consists of.

## Ways past a plateau

!!! key "Change the scenario when a score stalls"
    Swap in a different scenario for the same skill instead of grinding the one you've stalled on.
    Aimlabs warns that a routine with little variety holds back your progress.[^REF-9]

**Switch focus.** If your current weak subcategory has stopped moving, shift the bulk of your
[routine](routines.md) to a different subcategory for a while. Don't just grind the same one harder.

You may come back to the original one able to make faster progress, once it isn't the only thing
you're doing.

**Rest days.** Fatigue is one of the more common causes of a stall. So a deliberate stretch of
lighter training or full rest is a reasonable first thing to try, before restructuring anything
else. That's per the same overtraining research above.

**Sensitivity or scenario variation.** [Voltaic](https://blog.voltaic.gg/muscle-memory/)'s piece on
the "muscle memory" myth lists a deliberate sensitivity change among the ways players work past a
stall.

The logic is that a new sensitivity puts you back where there's obvious room to improve. Your hand
hasn't already fully adjusted to it. See [Sensitivity](../getting-started/sensitivity.md) for when
that trade-off is worth making.

The same logic extends to swapping in different scenarios for the same skill. A new one forces your
hand to adapt again, instead of coasting on one you've already mastered.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a single bad benchmark run as proof you've plateaued, or a single good one as proof
  you've broken through.
- Responding to a fatigue-driven plateau by training harder instead of resting.
- Repeating the exact same scenarios indefinitely and assuming a flat score means you've hit your
  ceiling, rather than that your practice has stopped varying. See
  [Myths](../myths.md#a-plateau-means-you-have-hit-your-ceiling).

**Do this next.** If you feel run down, take a stretch of lighter training first. If not, shift your
[routine](routines.md)'s focus to a different subcategory for a while.
