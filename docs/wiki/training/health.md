Long aiming sessions put steady, repetitive strain on your eyes, hands, wrists, and neck. It's the
same strain anyone working at a screen and mouse for hours gets.

None of it is specific to aim training: it's
general ergonomics and rest advice from health and ergonomics organizations.

- **Take regular breaks.** A few minutes away from the screen once an hour is a common pattern.
- **Warm up before hard sessions.** Make it part of the routine, not an optional extra.
- **Stop at pain, tingling, or numbness.** Scale training back, then ease it up again.
- **Protect your sleep.** Prolonged short sleep slows the reaction time you're training.

## Explanation

**Regular breaks.** OSHA's guidance for computer workstations calls for pausing computer use
periodically, rather than working in one long unbroken block.[^REF-041]

A commonly cited pattern is stepping away from the screen for a few minutes once an hour. That gives
whatever you've held in one position all session a chance to loosen up again.[^REF-041]

For your eyes, the American Academy of Ophthalmology recommends periodically looking at something
farther off, instead of staring at one fixed distance for hours. It's often summarized as the 20-20-20 rule: roughly 20 feet away, for about 20 seconds, every 20
minutes.[^REF-022]

**Warm-up before intense sessions.** Long stretches of repetitive mouse movement and static posture
build up strain on the same joints and tendons, session after session.
[Aimlabs](../resources/trainers/aimlabs.md) argues a short stretch or warm-up belongs inside your
training routine itself, not as an optional extra bolted on.[^REF-006]

**Posture and desk setup.** Wrist position, arm support, and screen distance all affect how much
strain a long session puts on your body. [Setup and Gear](../getting-started/setup.md) covers them
in more detail.

Mayo Clinic's office ergonomics guide also flags the mouse specifically:

- Keep it on the same surface as your keyboard.
- Keep it close enough that you're not reaching for it.
- Avoid bending your wrist up, down, or sideways while you use it.[^REF-037]

**Signs of strain.** Pain, tingling, numbness, or a dull ache in your hand, wrist, forearm, or
shoulder are common early symptoms of repetitive strain injury. They typically build up gradually
rather than appearing suddenly.[^REF-040]

Catching these early and backing off matters more than pushing through them.

The same NHS guidance also warns against the opposite overcorrection: stopping activity outright for
an extended period. An under-used body part can end up stiffer and less capable than before.

So the aim is to scale training back and ease it up again, not to abandon it indefinitely.

**Sleep and rest days.** Sleep affects the same reaction-time and visual-processing systems that aim
training relies on.

[Voltaic's own lifestyle guide](https://blog.voltaic.gg/lifestyle/) cites research tying prolonged
short sleep to reaction times comparable to mild alcohol impairment. It recommends roughly 8 to 9
hours, on a consistent schedule.

Full rest days between hard training blocks matter for the same reason a break inside a session
does. Recovery is part of how both the skill and the body actually adapt, not time taken away from
training.

## Common mistakes

- Treating strain symptoms like tingling or numbness as something to train through, instead of a
  signal to stop and rest.
- Skipping warm-up and breaks on the days you push hardest, exactly when repetitive load is highest.
- Cutting sleep to fit in more practice time, which works against the reaction time you're training.

**Do this next.** In your next session, step away from the screen for a few minutes once an hour.
