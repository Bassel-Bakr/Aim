An aim trainer is standalone software built around drills that isolate specific mouse-control
skills, instead of a full game.

- **You don't need one.** In-game practice modes work too.
- **A trainer gives you control.** You choose exactly what you practice and how you measure it.
- **Which trainer matters less than how you train.** Deliberate practice works in any of them.
- **Switching later is cheap.** The skills are the same in every trainer.

## Explanation

**Why use an aim trainer at all.** In a real game, the pace, target size, and target behavior are
whatever that moment of the match gives you.

Dedicated trainers build scenarios that isolate one category at a time. One might test only
[tracking](../categories/tracking.md), another only fast [switching](../categories/switching.md).
That lets you work on a specific weakness instead of whatever a match happens to throw at you.

The trade-off: a scenario is not a real engagement. Trainers isolate mechanics. A match also
demands game sense, positioning, and adjusting to abilities and recoil, which a scenario does not
reproduce. See [Transfer to Games](../fundamentals/transfer-to-games.md) for what does and doesn't
carry over.

**Dedicated trainer versus in-game practice.** Your game's deathmatch, practice range, and custom
modes have one edge a trainer can't fully match. They use your real crosshair, recoil, movement,
and hitboxes, so practice there transfers directly.

The trade-off is that you can't isolate a single category the way a scenario does. Your reps are
mixed in with everything else happening in that mode.

Structured routines often split the two. Per its
[Getting Started guide](https://blog.voltaic.gg/getting-started-with-voltaic/),
[Voltaic](../resources/communities/voltaic.md) organizes its routines into general Fundamental
Routines for mouse control, and separate game-specific ones.

The game-specific ones target the aspects of aiming each game uses most. That mirrors isolated
trainer practice versus in-game application.

**What a trainer does not decide.** Which trainer you install matters less than whether you train
deliberately in it.

A [routine](../training/routines.md) built around a weakness a
[benchmark](../training/benchmarks.md) found will move your aim in any of them. A session of
whatever scenario looks fun will not. The [practice principles](../fundamentals/practice-principles.md)
that decide which one you're doing are the same in every trainer.

## Picking one

This wiki doesn't compare trainers here. What each trainer is, costs, and ships with lives on its
own page under [Resources](../resources/index.md). Those details change on the developers' schedule,
not the wiki's.

Read the pages for the trainers you're weighing up. Then decide against your own constraints:

- what you're willing to pay
- whether you want to be handed a program, or to assemble your own
- which [benchmark](../training/benchmarks.md) you intend to measure yourself against

Two things are worth knowing first. A trainer you will actually open beats one that scores better on
a feature list. And switching later costs little: the skills are the same, and the communities
publishing [routines](../training/routines.md) and benchmarks generally support more than one
trainer.

**Do this next.** Open [Resources](../resources/index.md), read the pages for the trainers you're
considering, and install the one you'll actually open.
